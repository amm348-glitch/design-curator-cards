Bauhaus (1919–1933)

The Bauhaus was a German school of art and design founded by Walter Gropius that sought to unify fine art, craft, and industrial production. Its guiding principle, form follows function, required that visual decisions serve practical and social purposes rather than decoration. Through its foundation course, the Vorkurs, students studied materials, geometry, color, and structure as the basis for all design disciplines. The Bauhaus rejected ornamentation and historical revival styles, favoring clarity, abstraction, and reproducible forms. Although closed by the Nazi regime in 1933, the Bauhaus established the framework for modern design education worldwide.

Examples: MoMA — Bauhaus Collection, MoMA — Walter Gropius

Not to be confused with: Art Deco, because Bauhaus rejected ornament and luxury in favor of functional, socially driven design.

Swiss Style / International Typographic Style (1950s–1970s)

Swiss Style, also known as the International Typographic Style, emerged as a modernist approach focused on clarity, objectivity, and structured visual communication. Building on Bauhaus functionalism, Swiss designers emphasized mathematical grid systems, asymmetrical layouts, and typographic hierarchy to ensure legibility and neutrality. Designers such as Josef Müller-Brockmann and Armin Hofmann treated typography as an objective tool rather than a form of personal expression. Sans-serif typefaces like Helvetica and Akzidenz-Grotesk became central to this movement, which aimed to communicate information efficiently across languages and cultures.

Tags: grid system · typographic hierarchy · asymmetrical layout · objectivity · sans-serif typography

Examples: MoMA — International Typographic Style, Cooper Hewitt — Swiss Design Collection

Not to be confused with: Minimalism, because Swiss Style is defined by systematic grids and typographic structure rather than aesthetic reduction alone.

New Wave Typography (1970s–1980s)

New Wave Typography developed as a reaction against the strict rules of Swiss Style while retaining its typographic foundations. Designers such as Wolfgang Weingart and April Greiman challenged rigid grid systems by introducing layering, varied spacing, and expressive compositions. Rather than rejecting modernism entirely, New Wave expanded it through experimentation and personal interpretation. Typography became an active visual element, emphasizing disruption and movement over neutrality. This approach marked a transitional moment toward postmodern graphic design, influencing later expressive typographic practices.

Tags: broken grid · typographic experimentation · layering · expressive typography · postmodern typography

Examples: MoMA — Wolfgang Weingart, Design Museum — Postmodern Graphic Design

Not to be confused with: Grunge Typography, because New Wave emerged from modernist typography rather than rejecting structure entirely.
